# Strategy Can Be Fun While Auto-Battle Is Boring

A game can offer meaningful deck decisions while the combat those decisions produce remains visually uninteresting. Treating these as one measure of “fun” hides an important product risk.

## Two separate feedback loops

**Decision quality** asks whether players understand trade-offs, form plans, and see how their choices influence the outcome.

**Spectator quality** asks whether attacks read clearly, impact feels consequential, and the rhythm of effects, audio, camera, and pauses communicates what happened.

Reducing combat speed may make actions easier to see without making them more satisfying. Adding effects can make combat louder without making its state more legible. Both are hypotheses, not guaranteed fixes.

## A prototype test

Keep the strategic choices constant while comparing a small number of presentation variants. Observe whether viewers can identify the acting character, the triggered ability, the target, and the consequence. Evaluate sound and camera cues as part of the same event rather than unrelated decoration.

Ask separately whether players enjoyed making choices and whether they wanted to watch the resulting fight. Record which moment lost their attention. Do not substitute developer preference for external user evidence.

## A bounded presentation contract

A special ability may use a brief voice cue, a readable anticipation beat, restrained camera motion, an effect, impact sound, and a recovery beat. The sequence should reinforce gameplay information without blocking the next decision or overstaying its welcome.

## Limits

This is a validation framework, not a claim that any specific presentation sequence improves retention. Audio assets, timing, and camera movement still require actual in-game testing.

## Takeaway

Validate strategy and combat presentation independently, then check how they reinforce each other.
